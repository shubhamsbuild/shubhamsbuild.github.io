Question 1 A system built from zero

Describe one outbound system you built from zero. What was the company, what did the infrastructure look like when you were done, and what did it produce? Also tell us how many systems you've built this way in total.

An HR-tech company selling AI mock-interview software into higher-ed career services.

Nothing was inherited. No domains, no mailboxes, no list, no sequences, no reporting. Two cold-addressable segments came out of onboarding: career-services teams at small professional schools (medical, dental, law, business) plus athletic departments, and college counsellors at private and boarding schools.

ICP gate

Settled before a list existed, then enforced from data The client's own documents said three different things: under 1,000 students, 100–200 employees, under 5,000 students. The founder settled it: professional schools under 10,000 students, athletic departments exempt at any size. That exemption is not a fudge: their live athletics reference is a 60,000-student university, so the gate had to apply to the school, not the engagement. It was then enforced in the build, not in a prompt.

Lists

Scraped, not bought 2,067 unique independent schools from the national association directory, plus a merged 1,194-school set. The merge caught 234 duplicate rows that a straight concatenation would have shipped.

Sending

Domains and inboxes stood up from nothing Lookalike domains, mailboxes provisioned per domain, authentication per domain, warmed before anything launched. The primary domain was never in the sending path. Full spec in Question 2.

Reporting

Per-campaign separability as a design rule Segment, source and launch date encoded in every campaign name, so a dead segment shows up as a zero instead of dissolving into an account average. That naming convention is the reason the next slide's diagnosis was possible at all.

Question 2 Infrastructure, primary domain untouched

Walk us through exactly how you would set up sending infrastructure for a company whose primary domain must not be touched. Domains, mailboxes, authentication, warmup timeline, volume ramp, and what you monitor.

The primary domain is not a resource to be spent. It is the thing the whole programme is protecting.

What follows is the real spec from the largest build of this shape I have run: 50 lookalike domains, 100 mailboxes, roughly 1,500 sends a day at full ramp. Click through the six stages.

Lookalike domains, never the primary, never one of them

50 domainsbrand-adjacent, not typo-squattedprimary domain: zero sends

A reputation problem on a sending domain is survivable: you retire it. The same problem on the domain that carries invoices, password resets and the sales team's own replies is not. So the primary never appears as a sender, a reply-to or a bounce address.

Many domains rather than few, because reputation is per-domain. Spread across fifty, one bad list burns 2% of the estate instead of all of it. Names stay recognisably related to the brand, because the recipient has to believe the sender, and a character-swap of the real domain is the thing phishing filters are literally built to catch.

Two mailboxes per domain, pooled by who receives them

100 mailboxes2 per domainpools split by recipient ESP

Two per domain, not five. Mailbox count per domain is one of the cheapest signals a filter has, and the ratio matters more than the absolute number.

The pools themselves are split by who is receiving, not by who is sending. I built an MX classifier that resolves each recipient domain and sorts it into Microsoft, Google or a content-management host, then batches sends accordingly. The split is encoded in the campaign name, so the effect is measurable afterwards rather than assumed.

It has already paid for itself twice. It caught a gap in itself. It was missing protection.office365.us, the government-cloud signature, plus two mail-gateway signatures, and I found that while building a government-contractor list, before the send rather than after. And on one account it surfaced that Microsoft-hosted recipients were replying at 0.4–1.6% while Google and CMS-hosted recipients on the same campaigns ran 6–8%.

SPF, DKIM and DMARC per domain, then verified rather than assumed

SPFDKIMDMARC p=none → quarantinecustom tracking domain

All three on every domain, as a precondition of the domain being allowed to send at all, not as a fix applied after deliverability goes wrong.

Two failure modes I check for specifically, because both are common and both are silent: the SPF ten-lookup limit, where nesting enough includes stops the record evaluating and leaves a permanent soft fail nobody notices, and a DMARC policy at a partial percentage, which means the policy is only being applied to a fraction of mail and the reports are describing a sample. I wrote a checker for both, because they are exactly the problems that look fine in a dashboard.

A lesson about verification itself: my resolver died mid-sweep once and marked every domain unknown rather than erroring, so a run completed successfully with worthless output. Every DNS job now pins public resolvers, and a 100%-unknown result is treated as an infrastructure alarm rather than a finding.

Multi-week, and the honest answer is that it is a scheduling constraint, not a technique

built and warming before launch98 of 100 still warming at auditmulti-week lead time

Warmup is not something to be clever about. The only decision that matters is admitting how long it takes and putting it at the front of the plan, because the lead time is weeks and it cannot be compressed by wanting it more.

On that 100-mailbox build I audited the fleet and found 98 of 100 still warming, every one at zero sends, and reported it as a hard blocker rather than launching into it.

The thing that actually went wrong there was not technical. Every mailbox had been registered to one person, and the approved copy had a second person signing two of the campaigns. There were zero mailboxes for that identity, and new domains plus warmup is a multi-week clock. "Who is signing these, and do they have mailboxes?" is a two-minute question at onboarding that would have started that clock weeks earlier. I now ask it on day one. I also once told that founder the account had no sending infrastructure because there was no token in the environment file; it was already built and warming. I was wrong and I corrected it in writing.

15 a day per mailbox, held there

15 sends/day/mailbox≈1,500/day at full ramp3-send sequences

Fifteen a day per mailbox, which is deliberately unambitious. The ceiling is not set by what a mailbox can physically push; it is set by what keeps the domain alive in month six. Capacity comes from domain count, not from per-mailbox volume.

Sequences are three sends, as a house standard. The fourth and fifth sends in a sequence contribute almost nothing to positive replies and contribute fully to complaints.

The ramp is per-pool, not account-wide, so a pool can be held back or retired without touching the rest. Which turned out to matter: see monitoring.

Bounce per campaign, reply rate per pool, positive rate per source

bounce % by campaignreply % by poolpositive % by list sourcereply % by recipient ESP

Not open rate. Open tracking is noise now and the pixel itself costs deliverability. The north-star is positive reply rate: people who actually said yes, over people actually written to.

The catch that justifies the whole practice. On one account, two inbox pools ran the same campaigns, same lists, same days. Pool A returned 226 replies and 13 interested from 14,219 sends. Pool B returned 220 replies in one month, then 15 replies from 7,433 sends the next. That is a delivery failure, not a market one, and it is invisible on every reply-rate chart that averages the account. It also meant one vertical's zero was partly an artefact, because half that volume never arrived, so I said so instead of reporting the vertical as dead.

Monthly bounce is watched against the estate baseline of 0.81%. One month in seventeen ran 1.80%. That month is on the chart in Question 6, and I would rather you ask me about it than not see it.

Question 3 The two bugs worth the slide

Both of these fail silently, and both look like a copy problem eight weeks downstream.

1 · The pattern-guessed address

One provider returns a syntactically plausible address with certainty: not_found when it has no real answer. It is a guess at the format, dressed as a result.

If you do not null those explicitly, guesses enter the list indistinguishable from finds, and the bounce rate detonates three weeks later, by which point the list has been handed over and the campaign is being blamed.

2 · Catch-all is a domain property, not a verdict

Several major tools flag an address as catch-all while a different provider confirms the same address at highest confidence. The flag describes the domain's acceptance behaviour. It is not evidence against the address.

Rejecting on it zeroed out my best-performing tier entirely. Correct behaviour: accept, record the flag, and expose a switch for clients whose risk tolerance demands the stricter rule.

# Find and verify are separate commands on purpose. Verification spends
# credits, and credits are real money, so it will not run implicitly.
if args.verify and not args.yes:
    die("verification costs credits; pass --yes to confirm")

with singleton_lock(LOCK):          # two runs cannot race and double-spend
    for tier in CASCADE:            # first tier that answers, wins
        hit = tier.find(person, domain)
        if hit and hit.certainty == "not_found":
            hit = None                 # a pattern guess is not a find
        STATS[tier.name].record(hit)    # per-tier hit rate, printed every run
        if hit: break

Question 4 A play that failed

Describe a play you ran that failed. What was the hypothesis, how did you know it failed, how fast did you kill it, and what did you change.

Three different arguments to the same audience died together. That is what rules out the copy.

Hypothesis

HVAC owner-operators would respond to a tax-advisory offer The client sells to owner-operated businesses at $1M–$10M revenue. No vendor covers that market properly, so I built it: map searches on a self-subdividing grid, business directories, manufacturer locators. Roughly 60,000 trade companies and 27,000 professionals, zero vendor spend. HVAC was the obvious first vertical and I believed it.

How I knew

Three angles, 6,397 sends, three interested replies Not a near miss. The three angles were deliberately different arguments, run in parallel rather than sequentially, each as its own campaign. They died together. Three distinct arguments failing simultaneously is the evidence that moves the suspect from the copy to the audience. If it were the words, the three would not agree.

How fast

Within the first vertical, before the list was scaled Running the angles in parallel is what bought the speed. Sequentially, three angles at 2,000 sends each is three cycles of wait-and-rewrite. In parallel it is one read, and the decision was to change the audience rather than the words.

What changed

Lawyers in Texas: 28 interested from 23,284 sends Same account, same infrastructure, same data-acquisition machinery pointed at a different vertical. That one vertical produced 28 of the account's 44 interested replies.

Question 4 The fastest kill I have made

The best play I ever killed never sent an email.

A PR client's campaign premise: target Inc 5000 companies with no editorial coverage and offer to get them covered. Intuitively excellent, commercially obvious, and an assumption nobody had tested.

Get the list

5,002 companies, extracted past a bot-detection wall The source site is protected at the network layer, so fighting the HTTP layer was the wrong move. I pulled the data out of the page's own component tree in the browser instead.

Test the premise

A random sample of 100, audited by hand for existing coverage One afternoon of work on a question that the whole campaign rested on.

Result

96 of 100 already had editorial coverage The premise was true for 4% of the market, not the assumed majority. The real gap was not the existence of coverage, it was its tier.

What changed

Reframed from "we'll get you covered" to a diagnosis-led angle on coverage quality, before a single email went out An n=100 audit cost an afternoon and saved a quarter of sending against a premise that was false for 96% of the market.

I include this one because the honest answer to "how fast did you kill it" is sometimes before it ran, and that is a cheaper habit than being fast at reading failure.

If this goes further Week one

I would spend the first week finding out what your numbers actually say.

Not building. Reading. Every account on this page produced its result because something separable was measured (a pool, a vertical, a list source, a recipient ESP) and almost nobody sets that up before they need it.

Day 1–2

Audit what is already sending Domains, authentication, SPF lookup depth, DMARC policy percentage, pool separation, bounce by campaign. Half of what looks like a copy problem is one of these.

Day 3–4

Find the metric nobody is reporting Positive reply rate per list source. If auto-replies are counted in the platform's reply numbers, the noisiest source currently looks like the best one.

Day 5

One falsifiable hypothesis, run in parallel not in sequence Three arguments at once beats three arguments in a row, because when they die together you learn something and when they die one at a time you learn nothing for six weeks.
