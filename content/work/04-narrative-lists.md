## Problem one: the buyer is genuinely findable by job title

Enterprise sales reps at software companies. I sampled the audience and the titles
came back near-identical, over and over.

That is what a correct filter looks like. For this product the title really is the
qualifier, so the clever approach had nothing to add and the plain one was not
laziness.

## Problem two: the best campaigns were the ones that could not scale

Alongside the filtered lists I ran small ones built on a **story about the person**
rather than their job: sales people whose background suggests they would make good
founders, former professional athletes who moved into enterprise sales, people who
had just moved into a GTM hiring role.

None of those is a field in any database. "Used to play professionally, now sells
software" exists only in the words someone wrote about themselves, so the list has
to be assembled by reading profiles. That is why they are 56 and 36 people rather
than 500.

![Story-led micro campaigns converted at 9.7 percent against 2.7 percent for scaled cold outbound](/assets/exhibit-12-cedar-scale.svg)

**Then the account scaled, and the rate fell.** Five times the volume bought 40%
more interested replies. That is not a failure, it is the trade, and running both
in the same window is what made the size of it visible.

## The rule this account produced

Take something that got 7 replies from 56 people and rebuild it at 500, and the
story has to loosen until it means roughly "works in sales". At that point the
opening line is no longer true of the person reading it, and they can tell.

**If a list cannot be built at the size you want without weakening the attribute
that defines it, it is finished.** Go and find a different story rather than a
bigger version of this one.

## What it produced

**13 meetings in four days**, and 47.9% LinkedIn acceptance, the highest of any
account I run against roughly 10% on another.
