## The account

The agency's own new-business channel. Not a client engagement: this is the house selling
itself, on the same platform, measured the same way.

61 campaigns, 374,342 sent, **285 interested at 0.076%**, and **81 calls booked.**

Worth stating plainly and up front: **the in-house account converts slightly below the average
of the client accounts it is run alongside.** That is the honest reading and it belongs at the
top rather than buried at the bottom.

## The strategy the campaign names give away

Read the campaign list and the motion is obvious, because the campaigns are named after
clients: lookalikes of a loyalty-platform client. Lookalikes of a venue client. Competitors of
a capital-markets client, in three separate runs. A campaign aimed at the tax-advisory space,
built after two tax clients had been signed. A campaign named directly after an existing
account.

**The agency prospects using its own client roster as the seed.** Once an account is working,
two lists fall out of it for nothing: companies that look like that client, and companies that
compete with them. Both are cheap to build. Both are pre-qualified by the fact that the motion
already works for one of them. And both come with a case study you are allowed to reference in
the first email.

Against that sits the other half of the account: **cold vertical lists** with no relationship
to any existing client: a 109,000-row software list, an education list, a photography
list, recently funded companies, and one campaign named, with commendable honesty, *"Spray and
Pray."*

## The numbers

**374,342 sent, 5,116 unique replies at 1.37%, 285 interested at 0.076%, bounce 0.93%.
81 calls booked.**

| List construction | Camps | Sent | uReply | Interested | Pos% |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| **Lookalikes and competitors of existing clients** | 13 | 149,178 | 1,119 | **124** | **0.083%** |
| Cold vertical lists | 26 | 175,402 | 3,115 | 111 | 0.063% |
| Everything else | 22 | 49,762 | 882 | 50 | 0.100% |

**Client-seeded lists beat cold vertical lists by about a third**, on comparable volume. A real
edge and a modest one, and notably smaller than the source effects measured on the
client accounts, which run from roughly two-fold to twenty-six-fold.

The single largest campaign, an education list at 55,175 sends, returned 51 interested at
0.092%, above the account average, and a cold vertical list rather than a client-seeded
one. So the pattern is a tendency, not a law.

*"Spray and Pray"*, for the record: **12,840 sent, 4 interested, 0.031%.** The name was
accurate.

## What this account is actually for

**It is the control on the agency's own advice.** Every client engagement here argues that list
construction dominates copy. This is where that advice gets applied with no client constraints
at all, and the source effect turns out to be the *smallest* of any account in the set.

The most likely reading is that the client accounts have sharper ICPs. *"Companies that look
like our loyalty-platform client"* is a much broader target than *"attorneys in one state"* or
*"short-form creators in the finance category"*. The technique transferred. The sharpness did
not.

**57 of 61 campaigns count auto-replies in statistics**, so the 1.37% reply rate here is
inflated in the same way as the client accounts. Positives are unaffected, which is why the
table above ranks on them.

## What I would do differently

**The agency's own account should be its best-run one and it is not.** A below-average positive
rate, the same auto-reply defect it diagnoses for clients, and a campaign literally named
"Spray and Pray" that sent 12,840 emails.

**The lookalike motion was never systematised.** Thirteen campaigns were built off client
seeds, ad hoc, when somebody thought of it. Every signed client generates a lookalike list and
a competitor list by definition. That should be a step in onboarding, not an idea someone has
later.

**Nothing was learned from the education result.** The biggest cold list on the account beat
the account average, which is evidence against the account's own dominant strategy, and it was
never followed up.

## The transferable lesson

**Every client you make work is two free lists: their lookalikes and their competitors.** The
work of qualifying that market has already been done by the engagement itself, and the proof
you need for the first email is sitting in the account you just delivered.

And the one worth being uncomfortable about: **the in-house account converts below the client
accounts beside it.** These techniques were sharpened on client problems with hard constraints: regulated copy, an inherited ICP, no booking link, a fixed offer. Applying them to a
soft, self-defined target produced the weakest source effect in the book. The constraints were
doing more work than they ever got credit for.
